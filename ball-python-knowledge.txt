## Basic Facts
- Ball pythons (Python regius) are native to West and Central Africa; wild-caught imports are still common so always buy captive-bred (CB) animals
- Adults average 3–5 ft; females grow larger than males; lifespan 20–30 years in captivity
- Nocturnal and crepuscular; most active at dusk and dawn
- Name comes from their defensive behaviour: curling tightly into a ball and tucking their head inside

## Housing
- Hatchlings and juveniles: 20-gallon enclosure minimum; adults: 4 ft x 2 ft x 2 ft minimum (120x60x60 cm)
- Enclosure type: PVC, wood vivarium or glass with a secure lid; front-opening preferred as top-down approach triggers a prey response
- Two hides required — one on the warm side, one on the cool side; snug fit is important, they need to feel all sides touching
- Provide a humid hide (damp sphagnum moss) on the warm side to assist in shedding
- Substrate options: coconut fibre, cypress mulch, bioactive soil mixes; avoid cedar, pine and sand
- Minimum substrate depth 2–3 inches; deeper is better for burrowing
- Enrichment: cork rounds, half logs, fake plants, tunnels; clutter reduces stress

## Temperature and Humidity
- Hot spot (belly heat): 88–92°F (31–33°C) — use an under-tank heater (UTH) or radiant heat panel (RHP) on a thermostat
- Warm side ambient: 80–85°F (27–29°C)
- Cool side ambient: 75–80°F (24–27°C)
- Never let the enclosure drop below 72°F (22°C)
- NEVER use heat rocks — cause serious burns
- Thermostats are mandatory for any heat source
- Humidity: 60–80% ambient; 80–90% during shed; use a digital hygrometer
- To raise humidity: seal enclosure more, mist one side, use damp substrate, add a larger water bowl

## Feeding
- Prey: frozen/thawed (F/T) or live mice or rats; always offer pre-killed or F/T — live prey can injure the snake
- Hatchlings: fuzzy or hopper mouse every 5–7 days; size should be no wider than the widest part of the snake's body
- Juveniles (under 200g): small to medium mouse or rat fuzzy every 7 days
- Sub-adults (200–400g): medium mouse or small rat every 7–10 days
- Adults: medium to large rat every 10–14 days; overfeeding causes obesity
- Thaw F/T prey fully in warm water (105–110°F); never microwave; ensure no cold spots
- Feeding response: ball pythons are notorious for hunger strikes — especially in winter, during shed, after a move or hormonal changes; a healthy adult can fast for 6 months without harm
- Do not handle for 48 hours after feeding
- Remove uneaten prey after 30 minutes to reduce stress; try again in a few days
- Use feeding tongs or a separate feeding bin to avoid associating your hands with food

## Shedding (Ecdysis)
- Healthy adults shed every 4–6 weeks; juveniles more frequently
- Signs of impending shed: eyes turn blue/cloudy (in opaque phase), skin looks dull, loses appetite, becomes defensive
- Provide a humid hide during pre-shed and ensure humidity is 70–80%
- Do not handle during opaque phase; eyes clear about 3–5 days before shed
- A complete shed in one piece is the goal; retained shed (dysecdysis) indicates humidity too low
- To assist a stuck shed: 30-minute warm water soak, gentle removal with damp towel
- Retained eye caps (spectacles) are a veterinary emergency if two or more sheds are retained

## Health
- Common issues: respiratory infection (RI) — wheezing, mucus, neck raised; caused by temps too low or humidity too high
- Inclusion body disease (IBD): neurological symptoms (star-gazing); no cure, highly contagious; euthanasia recommended
- Mites: tiny black dots moving in water bowl, on snake, in crevices; treat with ProVetLogic or veterinary reptile mite spray; remove substrate, treat enclosure
- Cryptosporidiosis (Crypto): chronic regurgitation, weight loss; incurable; separate animals immediately
- Mouth rot (stomatitis): swollen, cheesy discharge from mouth; requires vet antibiotics
- Always take a new snake to a reptile vet for a faecal exam within 2 weeks of acquisition; many carry parasites

## Handling
- New snakes: do not handle for 2 weeks after arrival to allow acclimatisation
- Short sessions: start at 10–15 minutes, build up to 30–60 minutes maximum
- Never handle during shed, within 48 hours of feeding, or when the snake is in a defensive ball
- Support the entire body; ball pythons should feel secure; do not dangle
- Wash hands before and after handling; bacteria transfer both ways
- Hissing, tight balling, and hiding head are stress signals; respect them and put the snake back

## Genetics and Morphs Overview
- All morphs arise from selective breeding of genetic mutations; base animal is the Normal (wild-type) — brown and gold saddle pattern on black
- Genetics: mutations are either recessive, dominant, or co-dominant (incomplete dominant)
- Recessive: animal must carry two copies to express the trait; two copies = visual; one copy = het (heterozygous); hets look normal but carry the gene; e.g. Albino, Piebald, Clown, Desert Ghost, Ultramel
- Co-dominant (incomplete dominant): one copy shows a phenotype; two copies (super form) is a different and often more extreme phenotype; e.g. Spider, Mojave, Butter, Lesser, Phantom, Woma, Cinnamon, Black Pastel, Russo
- Dominant: one copy expresses the trait; no super form; e.g. Pinstripe, Spider (debated), Genetic Stripe
- Designer morph: multiple mutations combined; e.g. Bumble Bee = Spider + Pastel; Blue Eyed Leucistic (BEL) = Mojave + Russo or Butter + Russo (or other combinations)

## Key Single-Gene Morphs
- Normal: wild-type pattern; great feeders; recommended for beginners
- Albino (Amelanistic): lack of dark pigment; yellow and white with pink/red eyes; recessive; widely bred since the 1990s
- Piebald (Pied): variable patches of white unpatterned skin combined with normal patterned sections; recessive; pattern is random and unique per animal
- Clown: reduced pattern with a clear dorsal stripe and distinct head pattern; recessive; excellent morph for building combos
- Pastel: brighter yellows, washed-out pattern, lighter eyes; co-dominant; very common in collections; super pastel is more extreme
- Spider: broken pattern with a thin dorsal stripe; co-dominant; carries the wobble (see below)
- Mojave: reduced and brightened pattern; blue eyes in some combinations; co-dominant; super Mojave is a BEL
- Butter/Yellow Belly/Lesser Platinum: similar complex; used to make BELs; co-dominant
- Pinstripe: thin pinstripe dorsal line, reduced side pattern; dominant
- Cinnamon/Black Pastel: dark, saturated colouration; co-dominant; super forms (super cinnamon, super black pastel) are all-black and often have neurological issues — avoid producing super forms
- Woma: banded pattern, washed-out head; co-dominant; can have mild wobble
- Russo (Highway/Russo): pale yellow with dark dorsal line and muted sides; co-dominant; super Russo is a BEL variant

## Wobble Syndrome
- Spider morph and several related morphs (Woma, Hidden Gene Woma) carry a neurological condition called the wobble — head tremors, corkscrewing, stargazing
- Severity ranges from barely detectable to severely debilitating; stress and illness worsen it; no cure
- Affected snakes can live long lives with good care but some struggle to feed; quality of life varies
- Ethical debate in the hobby about continuing to breed Spider; many breeders have stopped
- Never breed Spider to Spider; the super form is not viable

## BEL Complex (Blue Eyed Leucistic)
- White snake with blue eyes; produced by combining two co-dominant morphs from the same complex
- Common BEL combos: Butter + Lesser, Mojave + Russo, Mojave + Butter, Lesser + Russo
- Not the same as albino (which has red/pink eyes); BEL retains some pigment in the eyes
- Highly sought after; widely bred

## Popular Designer Morphs
- Bumble Bee: Spider + Pastel; bright yellow with broken black pattern; carries wobble
- Coral Glow / Banana: produces yellow and lavender tones; male Bananas often produce 50% Banana offspring regardless of pairing (sex-linked); unique co-dominant
- Sunset: intense orange/red tones; recessive; used extensively in combos
- Leopard: reduces the pattern to thin lines; co-dominant
- Axanthic: reduces yellow pigment; black, white and grey; recessive; several unrelated lines (VPI, Marcus Jayne, Jolliff, etc.) that are not compatible
- Champagne: tan/peach with minimal pattern; co-dominant; super Champagne has neurological issues — same concern as super Cinnamon
- Scaleless: no scales on the body (retains belly scales); recessive; controversial welfare-wise — more prone to injury and infection

## Breeding
- Sexual maturity: females minimum 1500g and 3 years old; males minimum 500g and 18 months (but 2 years is safer)
- Breeding season: October to March (Northern Hemisphere); cool down slightly (75–80°F) to trigger ovulation
- Pairing: introduce male to female's enclosure; observe for copulation
- Follicles and ovulation: take female to a reptile vet for ultrasound to confirm follicle development
- Post-ovulation: female goes through a pre-lay shed; eggs are laid 30–45 days after ovulation; average clutch 4–10 eggs
- Incubation: 55–60°C (no — 88–90°F / 31–32°C) at 99–100% relative humidity; 55–60 days
- Hatchlings should not be fed until after their first shed (7–14 days post-hatch)

## Sourcing
- Always buy captive-bred from a reputable breeder; never wild-caught (WC) imports
- Reputable sources: Morph Market (morphmarket.com), specialist breeders, reptile expos
- Signs of a healthy animal: clear eyes (outside of shed), no mites, alert and responsive, no retained shed, no RI signs, feeding record provided, healthy weight
- Avoid: animals listed as 'not feeding yet', animals kept in poor conditions, impulsive pet shop purchases

## Tristan's Collection Notes
- Tristan Hall keeps ball pythons in Thailand
- Location affects humidity management — Thailand is naturally humid so desiccation is rarely an issue but airflow and bacterial growth need monitoring
- Local vet access for reptiles may be limited in some Thai cities; identify a reptile-competent vet in advance
- Local prey items (mice/rats) widely available in Thailand; ensure they are healthy feeder animals
